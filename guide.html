Reading The-Good-Guy-Daily

One web page, rebuilt every night on a small computer at home: a report on the US economy, then three markets measured against their own price trends: VFINX for stocks (an S&P 500 index fund), VBMFX for bonds (a total US bond market index fund) and gold. This guide explains how it is made and what every part is telling you.

Plain words the idea, no finance background needed Precisely exact definitions, data and method
What it isFour reports, one page

US economy first, then stocks (VFINX), bonds (VBMFX) and gold, each measured against its trend under its own blue band. The links at the top jump to each one.

Who writes itMostly scripts, a little AI

Every number is fetched and calculated by ordinary programs. A small AI model running on the home computer writes only the short news notes, and those are fact-checked.

WhenPublished around 3 a.m. Eastern

The data is the latest available overnight: yesterday's closing prices and whatever official figures were released by then.

How one night builds the page

The computer wakes itself at 1:00 a.m., does about five minutes of real work spread over two hours, publishes, and goes back to sleep.

  1. 01:00Wake upsystem

    The computer wakes from sleep and is held awake until the night's work is done.

  2. 01:05Library downloadscript

    New official documents from the Federal Reserve, Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and Congressional Budget Office (CBO) are saved to a local library, along with their text.

  3. 01:10Collectscript + AI

    A script fetches about 20 economic series from FRED (the St. Louis Fed's database), the agencies' release feeds, AP and Reuters headlines, and a short reading pack of new official text. The AI model reads that pack and writes a few short news notes in a fixed format.

  4. 01:30Direction calls, history comparison, morning notescript

    Rules turn the latest readings into five direction calls, compare today's conditions with every past episode since the 1940s, and write the "What it means" note. No AI involved.

  5. 01:40Fact checkAI, checked

    Each news-note sentence is put back next to its source text and checked one at a time. Wrong sentences are replaced by the source's own sentence; unsupported ones are removed.

  6. 02:05Economy report builtscript

    A second, purely numeric check drops any sentence whose figure does not match the night's data.

  7. 02:10Gold, VFINX and VBMFX reportsscript

    Full daily price histories are downloaded from Yahoo Finance, the trend lines are fitted and the charts drawn. About one second each. Weekdays only.

  8. 03:00Publishedscript

    The three reports are joined into one page and pushed to the website. A backup build at 06:40 and a second publish at 06:50 catch a late or failed run.

No night, no report.

If the computer is off overnight, the missed night is not faked in the morning. The next real run looks back over the whole gap and marks the report as a catch-up. A report that is more than a day old (four days for the price reports, to cover weekends) is labelled "not updated today".

Part one

The US economy report

A morning briefing built from official data. Read top to bottom, it goes from "what happened" to "what it might mean" to "how unusual is this, historically".

Morning note

Example, Nov 20, 2026: "Initial jobless claims (weekly): 203,000 for Nov 14, up from 202,000."

Plain words

The two-minute version. What changed lists the official numbers that came out since the last report. What it means puts them in context: are any recession warning signs on, and which way are things moving. Watch next shows the big releases coming up.

A green tick under it means every number in the note was checked against the night's data.

Precisely

  • "What changed" lists only observations whose release is newer than the previous report (not merely newer than the previous calendar day), with the prior value.
  • "What it means" is generated by script from the computed history pack: the warning-light count, the conditional recession frequency for months like this one against the unconditional base rate, and the five current direction calls.
  • "Watch next" is parsed from the agencies' official release calendars, with a cached fallback; the report warns if a calendar could not be read.

Where it's heading (direction calls and scorecard)

Example: "Inflation: up (high confidence) - CPI inflation y/y 3.72% for 2026-09 (was 3.35)."

Plain words

Five simple questions, answered every day: is growth, jobs, inflation, interest rates and recession risk going up, down or holding steady?

Each answer is a vote of three related numbers, so one odd reading cannot swing it. "High confidence" means all three agree. Each call carries its own test ("wrong if…") and is graded later, so you can see over time whether the calls are any good.

Precisely

  • Votes compare the latest reading with the previous one (a month earlier for daily series), with a dead band per series. Net +2 or more = rising, −2 or less = falling, otherwise steady; |3| = high confidence.
  • Growth: real GDP growth (SAAR), real retail sales, industrial production. Jobs: unemployment, initial claims, Sahm indicator (all inverted). Inflation: CPI, core CPI, core PCE y/y. Rates: fed funds target, 10-yr Treasury, 30-yr mortgage. Recession risk: Sahm, 10y–3m curve (inverted), Baa–10y spread.
  • A call stands until its direction changes or 35 days pass, then is scored RIGHT / WRONG / NO CHANGE on its key series. Results accumulate in the scorecard line.
  • These are descriptive momentum calls, not forecasts with a model behind them.

Key numbers

Example row: "Unemployment rate 4.1% · 2026-10 · ▼ 4.2 · 4.4"

Plain words

A dashboard of about 20 of the most-watched economic numbers: output, jobs, prices, interest rates, borrowing costs, the consumer mood, housing and oil.

Each row shows the latest value, the date it covers, an arrow for which way it moved since the previous reading, and the value about a year ago. NEW marks numbers released since the last report.

Precisely

  • Source: FRED series (BLS, BEA, Fed H.15, Freddie Mac PMMS, ICE BofA, Moody's, EIA via FRED); consumer sentiment from the University of Michigan's own page when it is newer than FRED.
  • Inflation, real retail sales and industrial production are y/y %; payrolls are the monthly change in thousands; GDP is the annualized quarterly real growth rate.
  • "Period" is the observation date, not the release date; the arrow compares with the prior observation (▲ up, ▼ down, ■ unchanged).
  • Spreads: high-yield = ICE BofA US HY OAS; Baa = Moody's Baa minus 10-yr Treasury.

Today vs history, and the recession warning lights

Example: "Recession warning lights: 0 of 8 on. Since 1986, in months with no lights on, a recession began within 12 months 2% of the time (4 of 241). Base rate for any month: 11%."

Plain words

This is the "has this happened before, and what came next?" section. It is pure arithmetic on long official records; no AI touches it.

Eight warning lights are classic recession signals, such as rising unemployment or an inverted yield curve. The fewer that are on, the calmer the picture. Each one is compared with how often a recession followed in the past.

Then every unusual condition that is true today (say, inflation above 3%) is checked against each past time it happened: did a recession follow, and did unemployment rise?

Precisely

  • Recessions are NBER-dated (FRED USREC), monthly data. Lights: unemployment +0.5 pt over its 12-month low; 10y–3m inverted; 10y–2y inverted; claims 20%+ over 12-month low; Baa spread +1 pt over 12-month low; industrial production below a year ago; housing starts 15%+ below a year ago; 3-month average payroll growth under 50k.
  • The light panel uses the common sample of all eight (1986 on), excludes months inside recessions and the last 12 months, and reports the share of matching months followed by a recession start within 12 months, against the unconditional base rate.
  • Fifteen conditions in all, each with its own history (some back to 1948). An episode counts when the condition begins; the table gives episodes, recession within 12 and 24 months, and the typical change in unemployment 12 months later, against the base rate since that series starts.
  • Small samples (often 3–16 episodes) mean wide uncertainty: "0 of 3" is weak evidence either way.

Official releases and headlines

Example: "Fed 2026-11-18 — Minutes of the Federal Open Market Committee, October 27-28, 2026"

Plain words

Two plain lists with links: what the government agencies published in the last three days, and the last day's economic headlines from AP and Reuters. Nothing is summarised here; it is the raw "what came out" so you can click through.

Precisely

  • Releases come from the Fed, BLS, BEA and CBO feeds plus the local document library (dated by release, so FOMC minutes appear about three weeks after the meeting).
  • AP and Reuters block scripts, so headlines come through Google News RSS. Titles only; their content is not used as data.

News notes (the AI-written part)

Example: "[DATA] Value added for outdoor recreation accounted for 2.3 percent ($704.2 billion) of current-dollar GDP in 2025."

Plain words

Short summaries of new official documents, written by the small AI model and then checked. Every note names its source, and every sentence starts with a tag that tells you what kind of statement it is:

[DATA]a number taken from the source [REPORTED]what the source says happened [OPINION]someone's view, as stated in the source [ANALYSIS]an interpretation, not a fact

A "corrected by fact check" mark means the AI's sentence was replaced with the source's own wording. "GAPS" lists what the source did not say.

Precisely

  • Model: a ~2B-parameter Gemma model run locally on the CPU. It works mainly from the night's reading pack of official text; scripts do the fetching and all saving, and the model's reply is parsed from a fixed format.
  • Fact check, one sentence per prompt with the closest source passages beside it: replaced with the closest source sentence if judged wrong, removed if unsupported, kept only if judged right and every number in it appears in the source, "unchecked" if the source could not be fetched. Separately, a script check in the report build drops sentences that misread the night's data.
  • Treat these notes as pointers to the documents, not as analysis. The numbers that matter are in the tables above, which no model writes.

History comparisons and open direction calls

Example: "T-3: Inflation: rising — confidence high; wrong if: the next CPI inflation y/y reading is below 3.72"

Plain words

The record-keeping at the end. History comparisons (S-numbers) spell out in sentences what the history table showed. Open direction calls (T-numbers) list each current call with the exact result that would prove it wrong, so the scorecard can grade it honestly later.

Precisely

  • Up to three conditions true today are written up, each with its episode count and 12/24-month recession frequencies.
  • "Wrong if" is fixed when the call is made (the key series and its baseline value), so a call cannot quietly change its own test.

Part two

VFINX, VBMFX and Gold vs trend

Three reports built the same way, with no AI at all. They ask one question: is the price today above or below where its past trends say it "should" be, over many different time spans at once?

How the chart works (illustration, not real data)

Solid dark line: the price. Dashed lines: one trend per time span, fainter when the fit is weak. Solid amber line: the all-history trend. Lines bunching together means the trends agree; fanning out means they disagree.

The trend idea

Example, Nov 19, 2026: VFINX closed at $663.15; its all-history trend says $557.30, so the price is 19.0% above it.

Plain words

Prices grow by percentages, so over time they curve upward. For each time span (the last month, year, 10 years and so on), the report finds the steady growth rate that best fits the prices in that span. That is the trend line.

If today's price is above a trend line, the price has run ahead of that trend; below it, the price is behind. Short spans show recent momentum; long spans show the big picture.

A trend line describes the past. It is not a prediction, and the report says so.

Precisely

  • Ordinary least squares of ln(price) on calendar days within each window: ln P = a + b·t. The fitted curve is exponential, a straight line on a log scale.
  • Windows in calendar days: 30, 91, 182, 365, 1826, 3652, 7305 (all three), plus 10957 (VFINX, VBMFX) and 14610 (VFINX only). A window with less than its full history (more than 7 days short) is flagged "short history".
  • Growth %/yr = e365.25·b − 1. R² is the fit of the log regression: near 1 the prices hugged the line, near 0 the window had no clear trend.
  • Each window is refitted from scratch every night. Fits are exact closed-form OLS, checked against an independent implementation to the cent.

The data behind each report

VFINX: 11,818 trading days since Jan 2, 1980. VBMFX: 10,062 since Dec 11, 1986. Gold: 6,582 since Aug 30, 2000.

Plain words

VFINX is Vanguard's 500 Index Fund, which tracks the S&P 500. Its prices include reinvested dividends, so they show what an investor actually earned.

VBMFX is Vanguard's Total Bond Market Index Fund, the first bond index fund for ordinary investors (1986). It holds thousands of US government, mortgage and high-quality corporate bonds. Most of its return is the interest it pays each month, so its prices also include that interest reinvested. When interest rates rise, bond prices fall, which is why bonds dropped sharply in 2022.

Gold is the price of gold futures on the COMEX exchange, in US dollars per ounce.

Both use the previous trading day's closing price.

Precisely

  • VFINX: Yahoo Finance daily history, adjusted close (dividends and distributions reinvested), rescaled so the latest adjusted close equals the latest NAV. Older points therefore sit below the NAV quoted at the time. Today's bar is ignored until 18:00 New York time, when the NAV is out.
  • VBMFX: the same method (Yahoo adjusted close, monthly income dividends reinvested, rescaled to the latest NAV), from the fund's first day, Dec 11, 1986, at a $10.00 NAV. The raw NAV has stayed near $10, so nearly all of the trend is reinvested income; the index it tracks today is the Bloomberg US Aggregate Float Adjusted.
  • Gold: Yahoo Finance GC=F, the continuous front-month COMEX contract, daily close; today's bar is ignored until 17:00 New York time. Roll effects between contracts are not adjusted.
  • "Next trading day" is the next weekday; exchange holidays are not modelled.

Summary line, dots and gap bars

Example: "BELOW trend on 6 of 9" with six red dots and three hollow green ones.

Plain words

The bold line at the top says how many trends the price is above and below. The row of dots says the same at a glance: red for below, green for above.

Each bar shows how far the price is from one trend: left and red is cheaper than the trend, right and green is pricier. Pale rows marked "weak trend" are spans where prices did not follow a clear trend, so give them less weight.

Precisely

  • Gap % = close / fitted value on the close date − 1, per window.
  • Bar length is linear in the gap and capped at ±20%; the printed number is exact.
  • "Weak trend" = R² below 0.5 (drawn at 40% opacity).
  • The all-history trend is reported alongside, not counted in the "N of 9" tally.

Price and trend lines chart, with the amber line

Example: on Nov 19, 2026 the gold chart showed the short-term trends ending above the price and the long ones below it.

Plain words

The last five years of price, with every trend line drawn faintly behind it, each running across its own time span and ending at the next trading day. The labels on the right name each line.

When the lines bunch together, short and long trends agree. When they fan out, the market's recent path differs from its long-run path.

The solid amber line is the trend through the whole price history, recalculated every night. The distance between the price and the amber line is the simplest answer to "how stretched is this market compared with its long-run path?"

On a phone the chart opens at today; swipe left for earlier years.

Precisely

  • Log price scale, so equal vertical distances are equal percentage changes and every exponential trend is a straight line. The y-range is the visible prices and line ends, padded 3%.
  • Price series thinned to at most 500 evenly spaced points for display, so a single-day extreme may not appear; the numbers in the tables use every day.
  • Line opacity = max(0.15, R²). Labels keep the order of the line ends and are pushed at least 10 px apart, so a label's height is approximate when lines converge.
  • Amber = the same OLS over the full sample (VFINX since Jan 1980, VBMFX since Dec 1986, gold since Aug 2000), refitted nightly: as of Nov 19, 2026, 10.2%/yr for VFINX (R² 0.96), 5.1%/yr for VBMFX (R² 0.94) and 9.1%/yr for gold (R² 0.86). A new day moves it by about 0.005%, so it drifts visibly only over months.

The table

Example row: "All history (since Jan 1980) · $557.30 · +18.99% · ABOVE · $557.45 · +10.19% · 0.96 · 11,818"

Plain words

ColumnMeans
Trend todayWhere the trend line says the price would be today
GapHow far the actual price is from that
On-trend priceThe price that would sit exactly on the trend next trading day
Growth/yrHow fast that trend climbs (or falls) per year
R²How well the trend fits, 0 to 1
BarsHow many trading days the trend is based on

Precisely

  • Trend today = exp(a + b·t) at the last close date; on-trend price = the same at the next weekday.
  • All dollar figures are in today's NAV (VFINX) or today's contract (gold) terms.
  • Short-window growth rates are annualized from weeks of data and swing wildly (for example −47%/yr on a 1-month window); read them as slope, not expectation.
  • The amber-bordered last row is the all-history trend.

What it is not

Not financial advice

The trends describe past prices. Markets can stay above or below any trend for years.

Not a forecast model

The direction calls and history tables count what happened before. They do not predict what happens next.

Not real time

Everything reflects the previous close and the official releases out by about 2 a.m. Eastern.

Not written by AI where it counts

Every number, table, chart and call is calculated by scripts. The AI writes only the tagged news notes, which are fact-checked twice.
